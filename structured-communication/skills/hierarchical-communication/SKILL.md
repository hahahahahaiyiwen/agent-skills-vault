# Hierarchical Communication

## Purpose

Make complex communication easier to understand, navigate, and remember.
Structure information already available; do not prescribe how the task is
reasoned about or performed.

## Core model

Use a tree as the primary reading path:

- The root gives the main message or answers the reader's primary question.
- Each lower level adds a useful degree of detail.
- Leaves contain concrete details, examples, or source evidence.

A pyramid is one rhetorical use of this model: lead with the main conclusion,
then group the support beneath it. Do not force every communication into that
form when the reader instead needs a sequence, comparison, or reference
structure.

### Vertical coherence

A child must have a clear relationship to its parent. Common relationships
include:

- **decomposition:** the children are parts or dimensions of the parent;
- **support:** the children give reasons or evidence for the parent;
- **procedure:** the children are steps toward the parent outcome;
- **causality:** the children are causes, effects, or contributing factors;
- **elaboration:** the children add examples or detail.

Keep one primary relationship under a parent. If the relationship changes,
introduce another labeled group.

Every child should roll up to its parent: removing the parent should make the
child's relevance less clear, and summarizing the children should recover the
parent's meaning.

### Horizontal coherence

Siblings should:

- be comparable in kind and level of abstraction;
- share an explicit organizing relationship;
- avoid accidental overlap or unexplained gaps;
- use parallel labels and phrasing where practical;
- follow an intentional order.

Useful sibling relationships include:

- categories or dimensions;
- chronological or procedural sequence;
- alternatives or contrasts;
- causes or consequences;
- priorities;
- claims and their supporting evidence.

Do not require strict mutual exclusivity or completeness when the subject does
not support it. Name meaningful overlap instead of forcing false categories.

### Cross-cutting relationships

Software concepts often form graphs rather than pure trees. Keep one hierarchy
as the primary reading path, then use links, identifiers, or short
cross-references for relationships that span branches. Do not duplicate the
same detail under every related parent.

## Apply the structure

1. Identify the reader's main question and the most direct supported answer.
2. Decide whether hierarchy will improve comprehension. If not, use concise
   prose.
3. Group information by the reader's task, not by the chronology of the
   agent's actions.
4. Choose the parent-child relationship for each group.
5. Make siblings parallel and choose a meaningful order.
6. Put overview before detail and keep evidence reachable from the claim it
   supports.
7. Stop adding levels when further hierarchy would not help the reader decide,
   verify, or act.

## Communication boundaries

When structuring a task outline or proposed understanding:

- label tentative interpretation as tentative;
- distinguish stated requirements from assumptions;
- do not invent scope, constraints, or acceptance criteria;
- do not turn an outline into implied user approval;
- do not add an implementation plan unless the task calls for one.

When structuring results or explanations:

- lead with the outcome or main conclusion when one is established;
- preserve uncertainty, failed checks, and material counterevidence;
- distinguish available evidence from inference;
- do not claim validation that did not occur;
- keep raw artifacts available by link or reference rather than copying their
  full chronology into the hierarchy.

This skill organizes communication only. It does not require additional
investigation, planning, implementation, or validation solely to fill out a
structure.

## Choose a representation

- Use short prose when there is only one main idea.
- Use headings and bullets for an overview-to-detail hierarchy.
- Use a table when siblings share the same attributes and comparison matters.
- Use numbered items only when order or sequence matters.
- Use a tree diagram when the relationships themselves are the subject.
- Preserve a user-requested or externally required format.

Use the fewest levels and sections that make the relationships clear. Allow a
reader to stop after the first level and still understand the main result.

## Quality check

Before presenting the result, check:

- Does the top level answer the reader's primary question?
- Can every detail be traced upward to a relevant parent?
- Is the relationship between each parent and its children clear?
- Are siblings comparable and intentionally ordered?
- Are overlaps, exceptions, and cross-links represented honestly?
- Can the reader move from a claim to its supporting evidence?
- Did the hierarchy preserve uncertainty rather than hide it?
- Would removing a level make the response clearer?

Do not describe this structural process unless the user asks. Present the
structured information itself.
