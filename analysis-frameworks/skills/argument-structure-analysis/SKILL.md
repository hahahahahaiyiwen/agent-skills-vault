# Argument Structure Analysis

## Purpose

**Stephen Toulmin's argument model** provides this skill's foundation. It
makes visible what is being claimed, what supports it, and what must hold for
that support to justify the claim.

This is a framework for interpreting justification, not a recipe for
persuasion. A well-organized argument can still have false premises, weak
evidence, or an unsupported inference.

## Core model

- **Claim:** the conclusion whose support is being examined.
- **Grounds:** the observations, facts, or reasons offered for that claim.
- **Warrant:** the connecting rule or assumption explaining why those grounds
  support that conclusion.
- **Backing:** justification for the warrant, distinct from additional grounds
  for the claim itself.
- **Qualifier:** the claimed strength, scope, or conditions of the conclusion.
- **Rebuttal:** exceptions or defeating conditions that limit the inference
  or challenge its applicability.

A warrant can be unstated. Making a possible warrant explicit in a review is
an interpretation, not evidence that the author actually endorses it.

## What makes the argument interpretable

- **Support traceability:** the reader can distinguish the claim, the grounds,
  and the bridge between them. A citation near a conclusion does not establish
  that it supports the whole conclusion.
- **Appropriate scope:** the claim does not silently extend beyond the
  population, conditions, or outcomes covered by its support.
- **Visible qualifications:** confidence, exceptions, and material
  counterevidence remain part of the argument rather than decorative caveats.
- **Identifiable gaps:** missing evidence, an unsupported warrant, and an
  overbroad conclusion are different problems; reorganizing the same text
  does not resolve them.

Deduction, induction, and abduction describe forms of inference, not components
of the Toulmin model. This framework can expose such support relationships
without choosing an inference procedure for the agent.

## Example

A proposal says, "Remote attendance will increase participation because
attendance rose in our pilot." The conclusion is the claim; the pilot
observation is the grounds.

The move from that pilot to future participation depends on a warrant about
comparability. Evidence that the pilot audience and conditions represent the
intended audience could provide backing. A restriction to similar conditions
would qualify the claim; limited connectivity could be a relevant exception.
Comparability alone does not show that remote access caused the pilot increase.

Those are possible relationships to examine, not missing facts to fill in.
A document review can identify the unsupported generalization without inventing
pilot evidence or attributing unstated commitments to its author.

## Boundaries

The model is not a formal proof system or a requirement that every passage
contain six labeled parts. No fixed prose layout, reasoning transcript, or
sequence of agent reasoning is required.

Preserve author intent and distinguish supplied premises from inferred ones.
Missing backing can remain missing; filling every component is not a reason
to fabricate support, discard counterevidence, or expand the task.
